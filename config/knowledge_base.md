## Exercise Ordering & Pre-Fatigue Management

Perform technically demanding, multi-joint, or heavier compound lifts before smaller
isolation or single-joint accessory movements. Fatigue and reduced motor control from
prior sets increase injury risk and reduce force production on the harder lift if it's
done later - this is standard resistance-training program design guidance, and the same
logic supports alternating opposing movement patterns (e.g. push/pull) within a session
so one exercise doesn't pre-fatigue the muscles needed for the next.

Source: NSCA (Haff GG, Triplett NT, eds.), *Essentials of Strength Training and
Conditioning*, 4th ed., Human Kinetics — https://canada.humankinetics.com/products/essentials-of-strength-training-and-conditioning-4th-edition-with-hkpropel-access

## Progressive Overload

Weekly training volume (sets per muscle group) has a graded, dose-response relationship
with hypertrophy - more sets generally produce more growth, up to a point of diminishing
and eventually negative returns from excessive fatigue. Progressively increasing weight,
reps, or sets over time, rather than repeating the exact same stimulus indefinitely, is
the primary driver of continued adaptation.

Source: Schoenfeld BJ, Ogborn D, Krieger JW. "Dose-response relationship between weekly
resistance training volume and increases in muscle mass: A systematic review and
meta-analysis." Journal of Sports Science, 2017;35(11):1073-1082 — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27433992/

Source: American College of Sports Medicine. "Resistance Training Prescription for
Muscle Function, Hypertrophy, and Physical Performance in Healthy Adults: An Overview of
Reviews." Medicine & Science in Sports & Exercise, 2026 — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41843416/

## Exercise Variety & Deload Cadence

A single one-week deload (roughly halving sets and frequency) at the midpoint or
endpoint of an 8-9 week block does not appear to hinder hypertrophy or strength
outcomes compared to training continuously through it, while plausibly reducing fatigue
and staleness - supporting periodic deloads and modest exercise rotation as a fatigue
management tool rather than a growth-driving necessity in themselves. This cuts against
constantly swapping exercises for novelty's sake; prioritize measurable progression on
a given exercise, and reserve deloads/variation for managing accumulated fatigue or
plateaus.

Source: Coleman M, Burke R, Augustin F, Piñero A, Maldonado J, Fisher JP, Israetel M,
Androulakis Korakakis P, Swinton P, Oberlin D, Schoenfeld BJ. "Gaining more from doing
less? The effects of a one-week deload period during supervised resistance training on
muscular adaptations." PeerJ, 2024;12:e16777 — https://peerj.com/articles/16777/

Source: Pancar Z, Ilhan MT, Darendeli MK, Karaca B, Ikidag MA, Tasdogan AM, Ulema MS,
Alkhamees NH, Al-Mhanna SB, Batrakoulis A. "Effects of deload periods in resistance
training on muscle hypertrophy and strength endurance in untrained young men using a
randomized within subject design." Scientific Reports, 2026 — https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-026-40612-5

## Hypertrophy Rep Ranges, Proximity to Failure & Technique

A repetition tempo of roughly 2-8 seconds and a range of motion emphasizing time under
tension at long muscle lengths both support hypertrophy; spending more time in the
lengthened portion of a lift can match or exceed full-range-of-motion training for
muscle growth in some cases. This favors controlled, technically sound reps over rushed
ones, and supports partial-range-at-long-length variations as a legitimate tool, not
just "cheating the range of motion."

Source: Androulakis Korakakis P, Wolf M, Coleman M, Burke R, Piñero A, Nippard J,
Schoenfeld BJ. "Optimizing Resistance Training Technique to Maximize Muscle Hypertrophy:
A Narrative Review." Journal of Functional Morphology and Kinesiology, 2024;9(1):9 — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38249086/

Source: Wolf M, Androulakis Korakakis P, Piñero A, Mohan AE, Hermann T, Augustin F,
Sapuppo M, Lin B, Coleman M, Burke R, Nippard J, Swinton PA, Schoenfeld BJ. "Lengthened
partial repetitions elicit similar muscular adaptations as full range of motion
repetitions during resistance training in trained individuals." PeerJ, 2025;13:e18904 — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39959841/

## Momentum & Strict Form vs. "Cheat Reps"

Using externally supplied momentum (e.g. body English, swinging) during single-joint
upper-body exercises neither helped nor hindered hypertrophy of the target muscle
compared to strict form in trained individuals over an 8-week program. This doesn't mean
momentum is necessary or advisable (it raises injury risk on some lifts and can be a
sign of a weight that's too heavy for the movement), but it means strict form isn't
uniquely required to build the muscle - form should be chosen for joint safety and
control, not because "cheating" the rep is assumed to blunt the hypertrophy stimulus.

Source: Augustin F, Piñero A, Enes A, Mohan AE, Sapuppo M, Coleman M, Wolf M,
Androulakis Korakakis P, Swinton PA, Nippard J, Schoenfeld BJ. "Do Cheaters Prosper?
Effect of Externally Supplied Momentum During Resistance Training on Measures of Upper
Body Muscle Hypertrophy." International Journal of Exercise Science, 2025;18(3):329-342 — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11970409/

## Hypertrophy Mechanisms & Realistic Expectations

Mechanical tension is the primary, essential driver of resistance-training-induced
muscle growth, acting independently of the acute hormonal response to a workout - the
post-workout spike in testosterone, growth hormone, or IGF-1 does not meaningfully drive
muscle protein synthesis or hypertrophy on its own, and "the pump" (metabolite
accumulation and cell swelling) lacks strong causal evidence as a growth driver by
itself. Realistic expectations: roughly 1-2 kg of fat-free mass gained over 8-12 weeks
of consistent training, with the rate of gain naturally slowing as training experience
increases - a plateau in that context is normal progress, not failure.

Source: Van Every DW, Lees MJ, Wilson B, Nippard J, Phillips SM. "Load-induced human
skeletal muscle hypertrophy: Mechanisms, myths, and misconceptions." Journal of Sport
and Health Science, 2026;15:101104 — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41276164/

## Shoulder-Friendly Pressing Alternatives

Wider grip widths and larger shoulder horizontal-abduction angles (the arm flared out
closer to 90° from the torso) on a bench press increase shoulder joint loading and
narrow the subacromial space, a mechanism linked to impingement-type shoulder pain;
narrower grips and lower abduction angles (e.g. a neutral or closer grip, or a
higher-incline/landmine-style pressing angle that naturally keeps the elbow more in
front of the body) reduce that loading. This supports favoring those variations, and
reducing bar path/grip width on flat pressing, for a shoulder that's irritable rather
than avoiding pressing movements altogether.

Source: Noteboom L, Belli I, Hoozemans MJM, Seth A, Veeger HEJ, Van Der Helm FCT.
"Effects of bench press technique variations on musculoskeletal shoulder loads and
potential injury risk." Frontiers in Physiology, 2024;15:1393235 — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11224528/

## Knee-Friendly Leg Training Alternatives

For irritable knees, machine-based, controlled-range exercises (leg press, leg
extension) are commonly used in place of free-standing barbell squats because the
resistance path and range of motion can be adjusted precisely and loaded progressively
without also demanding balance, bracing, and spinal loading - exercise therapy broadly
(quadriceps strengthening, hip strengthening, or a mix) is well supported for knee pain,
with different approaches producing similar small-to-moderate benefits rather than one
single "correct" exercise. Exercise selection should still avoid provoking pain that
worsens beyond mild, short-lived soreness.

Source: "Knee extensor training in patients with patellofemoral pain: a systematic
review and synthesis." (systematic review) — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12377044/

## General Warm-Up Principles

Evidence on general warm-ups (e.g. light cardio) before resistance training is mixed on
whether they improve subsequent strength performance beyond a specific warm-up (lighter
sets of the exercise itself); some protocols even show performance decrements depending
on duration and intensity. Separately, brief static stretching (under 60 seconds per
muscle group) included within a broader dynamic warm-up has only a trivial effect on
subsequent performance. Practical takeaway: a few progressively heavier warm-up sets of
the actual first exercise is a reasonable default, and brief stretching doesn't need to
be avoided out of performance concerns.

Source: Ribeiro B, Pereira A, Neves P, Marinho D, Marques M, Neiva HP. "The effect of
warm-up in resistance training and strength performance: a systematic review."
Motricidade, 2021;17(1):87-94 — https://revistas.rcaap.pt/motricidade/article/view/21143
